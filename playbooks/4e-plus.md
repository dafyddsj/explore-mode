# 4E+

Look at a digital learning experience from two sides. On one side is what designers control: the system and the affordances it offers through its interface (UI). On the other is what they don't control: the experience that emerges when learners engage with it (UX). 4E+ asks how that experience is embodied, embedded, enacted, and extended, and how affect, emotion, and aesthetics shape it. It then asks whether the system is organised to stay meaningful to its learners as conditions change, rather than decaying once the project ends.

**Why:** Learning technologies are often designed as one-size-fits-all, one-off interventions, and quietly go stale when their funding or champions move on. 4E+ treats learning as something that emerges from the interaction between learners and a system, so the design has to account for bodies, contexts, actions, tools, and feelings, not only content. Two cautions. The framework is conceptual: its source presents the autopoietic model as speculative and its benefits as untested. And the learner experience it centres can only be known from real learners. A model can generate plausible accounts of how people felt in seconds, and every one of them is fiction (**principle-ai-is-not-evidence**).

## Status and source

This is a draft built from one source: Aguayo (2023) (see **Source**). It hasn't yet been confirmed as the course's authoritative definition of 4E+.

- If the learner's course defines 4E+ differently, use the course's definition and language, and note the difference.
- Don't confuse 4E+ with unrelated "4E" models in other fields. Here, the four Es are kinds of cognition.
- The source uses "4E" and "4E+" interchangeably. In this playbook, the "+" always means the affective dimension described below. Don't extend it with other dimensions unless the course does.

## When to use

- The learner is designing, prototyping, or improving a digital learning experience, tool, or environment.
- Evaluating or comparing learning technologies, including AI tools, by how learners actually engage with them.
- Analysing why a learning technology engaged one group and not another, or stopped being used.
- Reflecting on the learner's own learning with technology, including where their thinking extends into AI tools (with **reflexive-thinking**).
- As a lens within `analyse`, `compare`, `review`, `reflect`, or `research`.

Not every technology question needs 4E+. A question about a tool's features, cost, or compatibility doesn't.

## The framework

**The four Es and the plus**, each read at the UI (what is designed) and the UX (what is experienced):

| Dimension | What it says about cognition | Design questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Embodied** | Thinking happens through the body and senses, not only in the head. | How do bodies meet the system: posture, grip, movement, comfort, fatigue, the senses used? Would a non-digital or hands-on element serve better? Does the hardware support learning or get in its way? |
| **Embedded** | Experience is situated in a social, cultural, and historical context. | Who are the learners, and what do their communities, histories, languages, literacies, and resources bring? What works here that wouldn't elsewhere? How are the designers' own contexts shaping the design? |
| **Enacted** | Meaning emerges from a history of acting and responding, a mutual coupling between learner and world. | What can learners do, not just view? Does the system respond to them, and do they respond back over repeated interactions? Can learners affect one another? |
| **Extended** | Cognition can extend into tools, artefacts, and settings. | Which devices, artefacts, or contexts become part of how learners think? What do they offload, and what happens when the tool is gone? |
| **+ Affective** | Emotion, affect, and aesthetic experience shape what is learned and why it matters. | What will learners feel, and why would they care? Does the experience connect to what matters to them? What aesthetic or sensory qualities invite attention, wonder, or discomfort? |

**The system side.** The source models a learning technology as a "learning actor" that should behave like a living system, maintaining and adapting itself while keeping its purpose. People are part of that system: teachers, designers, developers, and support teams, not only software. Three ideas carry this:

- **Structural coupling.** Learning emerges from recurrent interaction between learners and the system. Designers shape the UI; the UX emerges and can't be dictated.
- **Five design dimensions** shape the affordances on offer and must work together: education and pedagogy; the technology and platforms; design principles for the target affordances; the socio-cultural context; and complexity-informed principles.
- **Conditions for emergence**, drawn from complexity thinking in education: interactivity, diversity, ways for learners to affect one another, and decentralised control (in how the system is designed as well as in how it is used, for example open access rather than paywalls, team rather than single-person decisions).

## Method

1. **Confirm the definition and the purpose.** Check which definition of 4E+ applies (**principle-follow-applicable-rules**). Establish whether the learner is designing, evaluating, or reflecting, because that sets how deep each step goes.
2. **Name the system and its learners.** What is the learning technology, and who is part of it, including the people who maintain it? Who are the intended learners, and who else will end up using it? Who is left out?
3. **Separate UI from UX.** List what is designed: interfaces, hardware, content, prompts, physical or non-digital elements. Then list what is known about the experience, and from where. Give each UX statement an evidence status:
   - **observed**: from the learner's own research, testing, or data, with a record;
   - **reported**: from a source, with a source ID (**verify-sources**);
   - **hypothesised**: an expectation, including anything generated by EXplore.
4. **Understand the context before the interface.** For the embedded dimension, help the learner plan how to learn about the learners and their setting. Methods the source suggests include document analysis, spending time with the community and talking with local members, and reading local media. Engaging people may need permission or ethics approval (**principle-protect-data**). Where the context involves Māori people, knowledge, or data, apply **kaupapa-maori**. Where it involves other communities or cultural knowledge, apply **principle-cultural-awareness**.
5. **Walk the five dimensions.** Use the questions in the table. Ask a few pointed questions per dimension, not all of them, and let the learner supply what they know. Note where dimensions interact: for example, a headset that is uncomfortable (embodied) blocks the immersion it was chosen for (extended), and the frustration (affective) ends the coupling.
6. **Check the design dimensions and the conditions for emergence.** Is any of the five design dimensions missing or out of step with the others, such as a pedagogy the platform can't support? Which of the four conditions does the design promote or block?
7. **Ask how the system will stay alive.** How will it notice that conditions are changing, and which conditions matter for this purpose? Who maintains and updates it, through what routine (regular team review, periodic learner and teacher surveys)? Which design principles should hold steady while the rest adapts? What happens when the project or funding ends? If adaptation would be automated by collecting learner data, check that against **principle-protect-data** first.
8. **Return a profile with its uncertainty.** Give the learner the profile, the gaps in UX evidence, and the open design questions. The design choices and evaluative judgements are theirs (**principle-human-judgement**).

## Output

A 4E+ profile, as a table or structured list:
- For each dimension, the relevant UI features, the UX evidence with its status, and open questions.
- A note on the five design dimensions and the four conditions for emergence.
- A maintenance and adaptation note: signals of change, who responds, what stays fixed.
- The highest-priority gaps, usually "what we don't yet know about how learners experience this", with a plan to find out.

## Example

A learner is evaluating a VR field trip their school used for a geography unit. Engagement was high in the first week and fell sharply after.

- **Embodied:** EXplore asks how long sessions ran and whether anyone reported discomfort. The learner checks the teacher's notes: several students stopped after 10 minutes with motion sickness. That's **observed**.
- **Embedded:** the landscapes were overseas. The learner wonders whether local places would have mattered more. That's **hypothesised**; their own class survey could test it.
- **Enacted:** students could look but not act. The learner notes nothing in the experience responded to them.
- **Extended / +:** the learner records what students said they felt and remembered, from their interviews, not from EXplore's guesses.
- **Staying alive:** the content came from a one-off licence, with nobody responsible for updates.

The profile shows a design problem, a fit problem, and a maintenance problem that a usage statistic alone would have hidden. The learner decides which matters for their assessment.

## Distinguishing it from neighbouring methods

- **design-thinking** is a process for understanding needs and generating and testing solutions. 4E+ is a lens on how learners think and feel with a technology, and on whether it can sustain itself. Use them together: 4E+ can shape the questions asked in discovery and testing.
- **systems-thinking** maps actors, feedback, and leverage in any system. 4E+ centres the learner's lived experience at the interface, and asks a specific question of the system: whether it maintains and adapts itself.
- **reflexive-thinking** examines how the inquirer shaped the inquiry. 4E+ can feed it when the learner examines their own learning with technology: where their thinking extended into an AI tool, and what they felt.

## Watch for

- **Generated experience.** Personas, quotes, emotions, or "learners will feel…" statements produced by AI and presented as findings. UX statements without an observed or reported source are hypotheses.
- **Treating the theory as proven.** The source presents its autopoietic model as conceptual and its efficiency benefits as untested. Present 4E+-based claims about effectiveness as hypotheses.
- **Taking "living system" literally.** The autopoiesis idea is a metaphor for organisation and adaptation, not a claim that software is alive.
- **Designing affect as manipulation.** The source argues for designing for emotion and aesthetics. EXplore adds a limit: engagement isn't learning, and designing to provoke feelings can slide into persuasion or dark patterns (**principle-do-no-harm**).
- **Shrinking the "+" to looks.** Aesthetics here means sensory and felt experience, not visual polish.
- **Ignoring the designer's context.** Designers are embedded too (**principle-positionality**).
- **One-size-fits-all conclusions.** A profile is for a particular group in a particular context.
