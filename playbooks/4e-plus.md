# 4E+

People don't think only in their heads. They think through their bodies, within their communities and histories, by acting and responding, with the tools and settings around them, and in ways their feelings shape. 4E+ looks at how a solution works with all of that, or against it. The solution could be a lesson, a service, an app, a workplace process, a public space, a health programme, or the learner's own way of studying. Look at it from two sides:

- **What is offered:** what designers, organisations, or teachers control, such as the interface, layout, process, materials, and rules.
- **What is experienced:** what nobody controls directly. It emerges when people engage with what's offered.

Then ask whether the solution is organised to stay meaningful as people and conditions change, rather than decaying once its launch, funding, or champion has gone.

When the solution includes an AI system, such as a tutor, chatbot, recommender, or the learner's own assistant, add one more view. Thinking is then shared between people and a system that adapts and contributes but can't be responsible for what it does. See **When AI is part of the solution**.

**Why:** Solutions are often designed for an imagined average person, as one-off interventions, with attention to content and function but not to bodies, contexts, actions, tools, and feelings. They then fail for the people who don't fit, or quietly go stale. 4E+ gives the learner a structured way to see those dimensions. It also has two traps:

- **Invented experience.** The experience it centres can only be known from real people. A model can generate plausible accounts of how people felt in seconds, and every one of them is fiction (**principle-ai-is-not-evidence**).
- **Unproven ideas.** Parts of the framework are conceptual and untested, especially the idea that a solution can sustain itself like a living system. Use it as a lens for asking better questions, not as evidence that a design works.

## Which 4E? Definitions vary

There is no single 4E or 4E+. Authors and fields count and define the Es differently:
- Some work with three Es, and some with five or more, for example by adding ecological or affective dimensions.
- Some fold affect into the four Es. Others, like the default below, add it as a "+".
- Some unrelated models share the label, such as the 5E instructional model in science education (engage, explore, explain, elaborate, evaluate). That is a teaching sequence, not a theory of cognition.

A course may teach a particular version, but a course doesn't necessarily set one. The learner can shape their own perspective.

**Resolve the definition first:**

1. **Learner-defined.** If the learner has recorded their own version, use it. They might keep it in the workspace's `EXPLORE.md`, a course's `COURSE.md`, or a work item's `WORK.md`. As with other rules, the most specific applies to what it addresses (**principle-follow-applicable-rules**).
2. **Learner-supplied source.** If the learner points to a source or course material that defines it, use that definition as stated and cite it.
3. **Default.** Otherwise, use the default below (Aguayo, 2023). Say that you are using it, and offer to help the learner record their own.

A recorded definition can be short:

```markdown
## My 4E perspective
- Dimensions: <e.g. embodied, embedded, enacted, extended, affective, ecological>
- What each means to me: <one line each, in my own words>
- Sources: <who I'm drawing on>
- Emphasis or exclusions: <e.g. "treat affect as part of every E, not a separate +">
```

Help the learner build this with questions about which authors they find convincing and what they want the lens to notice. Don't write it for them (**principle-learner-authorship**). Where the learner's sources define a dimension differently, keep the difference visible instead of blending them (**principle-preserve-disagreement**).

## The default framework

Each dimension is read on both sides: what is offered, and what is experienced.

| Dimension | What it says about cognition | Questions to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Embodied** | Thinking happens through the body and senses, not only in the head. | How do bodies meet the solution: posture, movement, reach, comfort, fatigue, sensory load, the senses used? Who can't physically engage as intended? Would a hands-on or non-digital element serve better? |
| **Embedded** | Experience is situated in a social, cultural, and historical context. | Who are the people, and what do their communities, histories, languages, literacies, and resources bring? What works here that wouldn't elsewhere? How are the designers' own contexts shaping the design? |
| **Enacted** | Meaning emerges from a history of acting and responding, a mutual coupling between person and world. | What can people do, not just receive? Does the solution respond to them, and do they respond back over repeated encounters? Can people affect one another through it? |
| **Extended** | Cognition can extend into tools, artefacts, other people, and settings. | Which devices, documents, spaces, or people become part of how they think and remember? What do they offload, and what happens when that support is missing? |
| **+ Affective** | Emotion, affect, and aesthetic experience shape what people notice, value, and learn. | What will people feel, and why would they care? Does it connect to what matters to them? What sensory or aesthetic qualities invite attention, trust, wonder, or discomfort? |

**Sustaining the solution.** Aguayo applies Maturana and Varela's idea of autopoiesis, a living system producing and maintaining itself, to the design of learning technologies. A solution is treated as a participant that should maintain and adapt itself while keeping its purpose. People are part of that system: whoever runs, maintains, and adapts the solution, not only the solution itself. The source frames these ideas for learning technology. Applying them to other kinds of solution is EXplore's extension, so check the fit each time.

- **Structural coupling.** Outcomes such as learning, understanding, trust, or participation emerge from repeated interaction between people and the solution. Designers shape what is offered. The experience emerges and can't be dictated.
- **Design dimensions that must fit together.** For learning technology, the source names five: pedagogy, technology, design principles, socio-cultural context, and complexity-informed principles. In other contexts, identify the equivalents, such as purpose and method, the tools or infrastructure, design principles, the people and context, and how the solution handles change.
- **Conditions for emergence**, from complexity thinking in education:
  - interactivity;
  - diversity;
  - ways for people to affect one another;
  - decentralised control, both in how decisions about the solution are made and in how it is used. For example, open access rather than gatekeeping, and shared rather than single-person decisions.

## When AI is part of the solution

4E theories were developed to explain how human minds extend into bodies, settings, actions, and tools. An AI system doesn't fit them neatly. It adapts to what people give it, responds differently in different contexts, and produces new material, so it's more than a tool, but it isn't a person and can't answer for what it does.

Jho, Park, and Ahn (2026) examine this gap in their account of *ensemble cognition*. In their view, thinking in such settings is shared across people, AI systems, and artefacts, which together form an ensemble. They don't treat 4E as inadequate. They ask what changes when one participant is an AI.

Their analysis of the four Es, and their concerns about cultural defaults and responsibility, inform the questions below. Applying that analysis inside the 4E+ lens is EXplore's synthesis, not something either source proposes ([Foundations](../FOUNDATIONS.md)).

**Map the ensemble first.** Before walking the dimensions, note:
- **Who and what does the thinking:** the people, the AI systems, and the documents, devices, and settings they work with.
- **Who leads which part.** This can shift during an activity. For example, a person frames a problem, the system suggests options, and the person decides. Check whether the shifts are the ones the solution intends.
- **Where responsibility sits.** The AI influences outcomes but can't answer for them. The people who design, deploy, run, and use it do. Name them.

| Dimension | What changes when AI is involved | Questions to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Embodied** | AI draws on language shaped by human bodies but has no body. Knowledge from bodies, senses, and place has to come from people. | Which parts of the experience depend on bodies, senses, and place, and who supplies that knowledge? Is the AI being asked to stand in for understanding it can't have? |
| **Embedded** | An algorithm, not only people, can now reshape what people encounter. The AI also brings defaults from the context it was trained in. | Who changes what people see or can do: the people themselves, the designers, or the system? Who answers for those changes? Whose language, norms, and examples does the AI assume (**principle-cultural-awareness**)? |
| **Enacted** | The AI responds, but it doesn't engage with a world on its own behalf. The lead can pass between people and the system. | Do people still act, or mostly receive what the system offers? Who leads which part of the activity? Can people steer the system, and do they know how their input shapes what it gives back? |
| **Extended** | An adaptive AI isn't a passive extension like a notebook. It contributes, and its contributions can be wrong. | Is the AI working as part of people's thinking, or as a separate contributor whose output needs checking? What do people hand over to it? What happens to that ability over time, and can they still do it without the AI? |
| **+ Affective** | The AI's tone and confidence shape how people feel and what they trust. Its defaults can favour some ways of communicating over others. | How does the system's confidence affect people's trust in it and in themselves? Does it reward forceful, concise expression over careful, relational, or contextual ways of communicating? Who might feel their way of thinking doesn't count? |

If the learner uses their own dimensions, map these questions onto them, or ask the ensemble questions as a separate view. Don't switch to the default to use them.

**When the solution adapts itself.** An AI system that adapts automatically can look like the self-maintenance described in **Sustaining the solution**, but it isn't the same thing. Automated adaptation is a design choice made by people, and they stay responsible for it. Ask:
- What does the system change by itself, on what signals, and who reviews those changes?
- Who answers when an automated change excludes or harms someone?
- Do people and rules decide the system's role, or is it inferred from monitoring people? Example roles include a peer in group work, a mentor one to one, or stepping back during an assessment. Weigh the trade-offs: inferred roles need data about people and can misread them, while roles set by people and rules are easier to see and contest. Check any data collection against **principle-protect-data**.

## When to use

- **Designing** a solution that people will engage with: a course or lesson, a service, a product or app, a physical or digital space, a community or health programme, an event, a workplace process.
- **Evaluating or comparing** solutions by how people actually engage with them, not only by features or cost.
- **Analysing** why something engaged one group and not another, was abandoned, or worked in one place but not another.
- **Planning change**, such as introducing an AI tool, a new process, or a new space, and anticipating how it will land in people's bodies, contexts, routines, and feelings. When the change brings in an AI system, add the questions in **When AI is part of the solution**.
- **Reflecting** on the learner's own practice or learning: where their thinking is embodied, how their context shapes it, and where it extends into tools, including AI (with **reflexive-thinking**).
- As a lens within `analyse`, `compare`, `review`, `reflect`, `interrogate`, or `research`.

Not every question needs 4E+. A question about a solution's specification, price, or compliance doesn't.

## Method

1. **Resolve the definition.** Follow the order in **Which 4E?** and tell the learner which version you're using. Every later step uses the learner's dimensions, whatever their number.
2. **Set the purpose and mode.** Is the learner designing, evaluating, analysing, planning, or reflecting? What outcome is the solution meant to support: learning, understanding, wellbeing, participation, safety, or something else? The mode sets how deep each step goes.
3. **Name the solution and its people.** What is the solution, and who is part of it, including the people who run and maintain it? Who is it for? Who else will meet it? Who is left out? If an AI system is part of the solution, map the ensemble too: who and what does the thinking, who leads which part, and where responsibility sits.
4. **Separate what is offered from what is experienced.** List what is designed or provided. Then list what is known about people's experience, and from where. Give each statement about experience an evidence status:
   - **observed**: from the learner's own research, testing, or data, with a record;
   - **reported**: from a source, with a source ID (**verify-sources**);
   - **hypothesised**: an expectation, including anything EXplore generates.
5. **Understand the context before the solution.** For the embedded dimension, help the learner plan how to learn about the people and their setting. Possible methods include:
   - document analysis;
   - spending time with the community and talking with its members;
   - observation;
   - reading local media and community channels.

   Engaging people may need permission or ethics approval (**principle-protect-data**). Where the context involves Māori people, knowledge, or data, apply **kaupapa-maori**. For other communities or cultural knowledge, apply **principle-cultural-awareness**.
6. **Walk the dimensions.** Ask a few pointed questions per dimension, not all of them, and let the learner supply what they know. Where AI is part of the solution, draw on the AI questions as well, mapped onto the learner's dimensions. Note where dimensions interact. For example, an uncomfortable headset (embodied) blocks the immersion it was chosen for (extended), and the frustration (affective) ends the engagement.
7. **Check fit and the conditions for emergence.** Are the solution's design dimensions in step with one another, such as a method the tools can actually support? Which conditions for emergence does it promote or block?
8. **Ask how it will stay alive.**
   - How will it notice that people or conditions are changing, and which changes matter for this purpose?
   - Who maintains and adapts it, and through what routine, such as regular team review or periodic feedback from the people who use it?
   - What should hold steady while the rest adapts?
   - What happens when the project, funding, or champion goes?
   - If adaptation would be automated by collecting people's data, check that against **principle-protect-data** first. If the system would adapt itself, also ask the questions in **When the solution adapts itself**.
9. **Return a profile with its uncertainty.** Give the learner the profile, the gaps in evidence about experience, and the open questions. The design choices and judgements are theirs (**principle-human-judgement**).

## Output

A 4E+ profile, as a table or structured list:
- the definition used and its source;
- for each dimension, what is offered, the evidence about experience with its status, and open questions;
- a note on design fit and the conditions for emergence;
- a sustaining note: signals of change, who responds, and what stays fixed;
- where AI is part of the solution, an ensemble note: who and what does the thinking, who leads which part, where responsibility sits, and what the system changes by itself;
- the highest-priority gaps, usually "what we don't yet know about how people experience this", with a plan to find out.

In reflective use, the output is a set of questions per dimension instead of a profile, and the learner writes any reflection themselves.

## Examples

**Evaluating a learning technology.** A school's VR field trip had high engagement in week one and little after.
- **Embodied:** teacher notes show students stopping after 10 minutes with motion sickness (**observed**).
- **Embedded:** the landscapes were overseas, and local places might have mattered more (**hypothesised**; a class survey could test it).
- **Enacted:** students could look but not act.
- **Sustaining:** the content came from a one-off licence, and nobody was responsible for updates.

**Planning a service change.** A council plans to move community-hall bookings online only.
- **Embodied:** small screens and fine motor demands.
- **Embedded:** which groups book halls, and their digital access, should be checked against booking records (**reported** once sourced).
- **Extended:** many bookers relied on a staff member who knew the halls. That knowledge leaves with the phone line.
- **Affective:** losing a familiar person may feel like exclusion.
- **Sustaining:** who notices when a group stops booking?

**Adding AI to a workplace process.** A community legal clinic trials an AI tool that drafts first summaries of clients' situations for caseworkers.
- **Ensemble:** the client, the caseworker, the AI tool, and the case files. The tool now leads the first draft, and the caseworker edits and decides. The clinic, not the tool, answers for what the summaries say.
- **Embodied:** what a caseworker notices in person, such as hesitation or distress, isn't in the tool's draft (**hypothesised**; caseworker interviews could test it).
- **Extended:** if caseworkers stop writing first summaries, what happens to that skill, and to their judgement of a case (**hypothesised**)?
- **Affective:** fluent, confident drafts may make caseworkers less likely to question them.
- **Data:** client information going to the tool must be checked against **principle-protect-data** before the trial, not after it.

**Reflecting on the learner's own study.** A learner examines how they now use an AI assistant.
- **Ensemble:** which parts of their study they lead and which the assistant now leads, and whether that's the split they want for what they're trying to learn (**principle-learner-agency**).
- **Extended:** which parts of their thinking now happen in the tool, and what they can still do without it.
- **Embodied:** where and when they think best.
- **Affective:** whether the tool's confidence changes how sure they feel.

EXplore asks. The learner answers and draws the conclusions.

## Distinguishing it from neighbouring methods

- **design-thinking** is a process for understanding needs and for generating and testing solutions. 4E+ is a lens on how people think and feel with a solution, and whether it can sustain itself. Use them together: 4E+ can shape the questions asked in discovery and testing.
- **systems-thinking** maps actors, feedback, and leverage in any system. 4E+ centres lived experience where people meet the solution, and asks one specific question of the system: whether it maintains and adapts itself.
- **reflexive-thinking** examines how the inquirer shaped the inquiry. 4E+ can feed it when the learner examines their own thinking with tools, places, and feelings.
- **Ensemble cognition** (Jho et al., 2026) is a philosophical account of how thinking is shared between people and AI systems, and of where responsibility sits. 4E+ centres how people think and feel with a solution. The ensemble view looks at the whole arrangement of people and AI. Use it inside 4E+ when AI is part of the solution.

  The two differ on one point. Keep both views visible rather than blending them (**principle-preserve-disagreement**). 4E+'s sustaining idea treats the solution, including the people who run it, as a participant that maintains itself. The ensemble view holds that AI doesn't engage with a world on its own behalf and can't be responsible for what it does. Ask the learner how they read the difference for their solution. Either way, responsibility for automated adaptation stays with people.

## Watch for

- **Generated experience.** Personas, quotes, emotions, or "people will feel…" statements produced by AI and presented as findings. Experience claims without an observed or reported source are hypotheses.
- **Imposing a definition.** Using the default when the learner has their own, or treating any one version of 4E as the correct one.
- **Treating the theory as proven.** Present 4E+-based claims about effectiveness as hypotheses. The autopoietic model is explicitly conceptual in its source, and stretching it beyond learning technology adds another layer of assumption.
- **Taking "living system" literally.** Autopoiesis is used here as a metaphor for organisation and adaptation.
- **Designing affect as manipulation.** The source argues for designing with emotion and aesthetics in mind. EXplore adds a limit: engagement isn't the goal in itself, and designing to provoke feelings can slide into persuasion or dark patterns (**principle-do-no-harm**).
- **Shrinking the "+" to looks.** Aesthetics here means sensory and felt experience, not visual polish.
- **Ignoring the designer's context.** Designers, organisations, and the learner are embedded too (**principle-positionality**).
- **One-size-fits-all conclusions.** A profile is for particular people in a particular context.
- **Forcing the lens.** If a dimension adds nothing for this question, say so and move on.
- **Treating the AI as one of the people.** An AI system's account of what users feel or need is generated, not experienced. It's a hypothesis, like any other invented experience.
- **Treating the AI as a neutral tool.** Its defaults, framing, and automatic adaptations shape the experience. Ask whose they are, and who answers for them.

- **Access:** read in full from a PDF supplied by the playbook's author.
- **Not yet read:** the open peer commentaries and author's response published with the article. They may qualify or contest the framework.
- **Scope:** the source applies the framework to learning technology. Its use for wider solutions and contexts here is EXplore's extension.
- **Underlying work:** the source draws on the 4E cognition literature (Newen, A., De Bruin, L., & Gallagher, S. (Eds.) (2018), *The Oxford Handbook of 4E Cognition*, Oxford University Press), and on Maturana and Varela's concepts of autopoiesis and structural coupling. The conditions for emergence come from Davis and Sumara (2005), as cited in the source.

AI lens: Jho, H., Park, C., & Ahn, D. (2026). Towards a philosophy of ensemble cognition: Reconceptualising agency and mind in AI-mediated educational environments. *Educational Philosophy and Theory*, 58(11), 1035–1054. https://doi.org/10.1080/00131857.2026.2654678

- **Access:** full text read from the open-access EPUB (CC BY 4.0).
- **Scope:** a conceptual paper. It examines 4E cognition, distributed cognition, and actor-network theory as theories of mind in AI-mediated education, and it proposes ensemble cognition. It doesn't discuss 4E+ or autopoiesis, or offer a method for designing solutions. Using its questions within the 4E+ lens is EXplore's synthesis.
- **Limits:** the authors say the framework is untested, and that it comes from Western philosophy and may not fit every educational culture.
